Spatial disorientation illusions

Spatial disorientation is when a pilot's sense of position, motion, or attitude (how the aircraft is tilted) doesn't match reality. Pick an illusion, then press Next to step through it. The left dial shows what the aircraft is really doing, and the right dial shows what the pilot feels.

Learning objective

Name four spatial disorientation illusions and explain why trusting the instruments is the standard defence.

    Key takeaway

    Without clear visual cues, the inner ear can give a pilot a convincing but wrong sense of motion. The defence is training and instrument discipline, not instinct.

    Think about it

    Which of these illusions is most likely on a night takeoff with no visible horizon? What could an operator do to reduce the risk?

    Show a discussion point

    The somatogravic illusion: strong acceleration feels like the nose pitching up. Operators can reduce the risk with instrument-focused takeoff procedures, regular simulator practice, and crew monitoring of pitch and altitude.

    For learning only. The angles and timings are illustrative, not exact. In real flight, the lesson is the same for every illusion: trust the instruments over your body's sensations. This tool must not be used for real flight-safety decisions.
